# Karpathy Wiki vs OpenBrain - Memory and Context Layer Systems

> Source: YouTube video by Nate (creator of OpenBrain). Transcript supplied by Mick, organised into a structured note.
> URL: https://youtu.be/dxq7WtWxi44
> Core theme: comparing Andrej Karpathy's personal-wiki idea against Nate's OpenBrain system, and how to get the best of both.

---

## 1. Why this matters

Karpathy posted a "wiki" idea a couple of days ago; 41,000 people bookmarked it. On the surface it is ridiculously simple: use your AI to build and maintain a personal wiki.

Nate launched something similar with OpenBrain, and has had hundreds of DMs asking whether the wiki is the same thing, whether it makes OpenBrain obsolete, and which is better.

His answer: deciding how you organise your context layer is one of the single most important things you can do in 2026. The two approaches are genuinely different, chosen for different reasons. This is not a steelman and there is no easy winner - the aim is to show where each one is strong, where each one breaks, the scale issues of each, and then how to get the best of both worlds (OpenBrain now ships a plugin that combines the wiki approach with structured data).

---

## 2. Karpathy's wiki - the core insight

On the surface Karpathy's implementation is just folders and text files. Why so simple?

**The problem he is solving:** everyone uses AI with their documents all day (ChatGPT, NotebookLM, Claude). You end up with documents scattered everywhere, ask a question, and the AI hunts down relevant chunks and previous chats, reads them, and synthesises an answer. It works, but it is not ideal - the AI effectively rediscovers your knowledge from scratch every single time.

- Ask a question needing five documents across six chats: the AI finds all five, reads the six chats, works out how they relate, produces a synthesis.
- Ask something similar tomorrow: it does the whole thing again. Nothing about that synthesis was saved. Connections between documents are not preserved - even in the same app, let alone across two or three different AIs.
- In other words, the AI did real cognitive work and threw it all away.

**Karpathy's question:** what if, instead of just finding chunks and answering, the AI actually wrote down what it learned? Every time you add a source, the AI reads it, works out what matters, and updates a set of organised notes that already contain everything it learned from previous sources. The notes auto-update from the AI's own synthesis - with cross-references, flagged contradictions, and a record of how your understanding evolved.

The wiki becomes not just clever file organisation but a persistent artifact capturing the AI's evolving understanding of your thinking over time. Read a paper Monday, write up what was learned, link it back to last week's thread. Ask a question Friday and the AI does not re-read everything - it looks at the synthesis, cross-references, and contradictions already sitting there.

**Key phrase (Karpathy):** the knowledge is "compiled once and then kept current" - it is not re-derived on every query. Most AI knowledge tools spend compute and tokens to re-derive; his wiki compiles. That is a fundamentally different relationship between AI and your information, with different strengths and weaknesses.

### The setup

- AI agent on one side, Obsidian (a note-viewing app) on the other.
- The AI makes edits based on the conversation; the human browses results in real time, follows links, checks the graph view, reads updated pages, and has an evolving conversation with the agent.
- Karpathy (a programmer) frames the LLM as the programmer for the "codebase" of the wiki. In plain English: the note app is where he reads, the AI is what writes into it, from a collected series of built documents (raw sources he inputs from previous chats, docs, and AI-produced synthesis).

**Analogy:** most AI tools are a brilliant research assistant who reads your entire filing cabinet for every question, gives a great answer, then immediately forgets everything. Karpathy's wiki is that same assistant keeping a running, organised, cross-referenced, updated set of notes so each question builds on the last instead of starting over. That "building on your learnings" is why 41,000 people bookmarked it - an AI that builds understanding over time is what everyone is hungry for. It is also what made people excited about OpenBrain.

## 3. The catch nobody in the 41,000 bookmarks is thinking about

Every time the AI turns a raw source into a wiki page it makes editorial decisions - how to frame connections between ideas, which may be right or wrong. Those are the AI's choices, not the human's. Important nuance can get quietly dropped and you would never know, because the wiki reads so cleanly.

This is the dashboard trap: a dashboard is easier to read than a spreadsheet, but it is a condensation of data and can hide exactly the thing you need to see.

To his credit, Karpathy keeps all raw sources untouched in their own folders, so you can always go back to the originals - a smart design. But in practice most people building on the pattern will not maintain the discipline to return to raw sources. The wiki becomes the trusted thing, and the source of truth quietly shifts from raw material to an AI summary of it. That summary may be correct 80-90% of the time, but where it misses or slightly mis-frames something, the error gets baked into your understanding - and you will not get in the habit of questioning it, because the whole premise is that we are lazy and it is nice to just chuck stuff in and have it auto-organise.

## 4. The fundamental fork: when does the AI do the hard thinking?

Every AI-at-its-core knowledge system must answer one question: does the AI do the hard thinking when information comes IN, or when you ASK about it? Everything else follows from that fork.

### Karpathy's wiki = a write-time (input-time) system

- When a new source arrives, the AI does not just store it - it actively works against it. It reads the source, extracts what matters, and writes that understanding into the wiki.
- It updates topic pages, writes summaries, adds links between related ideas, develops concepts, and notes where new data contradicts something filed last week.
- The hard work happens once, at the moment information comes in the door. After that, browsing the wiki gives pre-built understanding with almost no further AI work - it is just retrieving.

### OpenBrain = a query-time system

- When new information arrives, OpenBrain stores it faithfully: tags it, categorises it, makes it searchable. Nothing is synthesised yet. Data sits in structured tables, waiting.
- When you (or your AI agent) ask a question, that is when the AI goes to work: it reads the relevant entries, thinks fresh, and produces an answer on the fly.
- The hard work happens at the moment you need it, not before.

### The two metaphors

- **Karpathy's wiki = a study guide** a great tutor writes for you as you learn. Every time you cover new material the tutor updates the guide, adds sections, revises old ones, connects ideas across chapters. Come exam day you just read the guide. The thinking has been done for you in advance.
- **OpenBrain = a perfectly organised filing cabinet with a brilliant librarian** beside it. Every document filed, indexed, searchable. When you need an answer the librarian quickly pulls the relevant file, reads it, and pinpoints exactly what you need - doing the thinking fresh and efficiently every time, because the filing is clean.

Both are useful; they are good at different things. There is no easy winner.

## 5. Why the fork matters in practice - the two failure modes

- **If you only STORE stuff** (store-only), the AI must figure out what it all means every time you ask. After months of feeding it articles, meeting notes, and research, a question that connects many sources forces it to burn tokens finding, reading, relating, and synthesising from scratch - every single time. Nothing was pre-built.
- **If you only build a WIKI** (wiki-only), the AI can read the summary but cannot do anything precise with the raw data underneath. It cannot answer "pull every deal over $50,000 from last quarter", "filter all meetings by client name", or serve three AI tools querying the knowledge base at once. A folder of text files cannot answer complex structured questions - the detailed structured data is not there, by design.

### Multi-agent and structured data

- With three or more agents, a wiki breaks when they all try to write Markdown files at once. The wiki structure presupposes a single agent writing in one place.
- OpenBrain's structure assumes you may want multiple agents hooking in at multiple points to contribute to or pull from a structured database.

## 6. Facts vs narrative - and why contradictions are precious

Right now it is hard to trace how an AI learns or improves over time when there is no memory architecture underneath.

- **OpenBrain remembers detailed facts.**
- **The wiki remembers narrative / synthesis.**

Storage architecture shapes the future you unlock for a team - you are effectively choosing the context layer you have to make sense of, trust, and depend on for decisions. The stakes could not be higher. Most organisations generate enormous volumes of AI-touched knowledge (meeting summaries, strategy docs, research outputs, Slack threads) and almost all of it is write-once, read-never because nobody maintains or synthesises across it. Your company's AI-generated knowledge is either a compounding asset or a growing pile of noise.

**Contradictions can be the most valuable thing in your knowledge base.** Example: engineering thinks the build timeline is 12 weeks; sales promised the client 8. A "smart" wiki might resolve that into one coherent narrative - splitting the difference at an estimated 10 weeks - and smooth away the very misalignment leadership needs to see. A database that stores both views without resolving them preserves that strategic tension. A well-meaning wiki can erase it.

---

## 7. The AI's job description differs

- **In Karpathy's system the AI is primarily a WRITER.** Its job is to maintain a document. Each new source means reading raw material, synthesising it, writing an opinion, updating wiki pages, connecting links, adding concept explanations, cross-referencing, building an index. It is editorial work - judgment calls about what is important, what connects to what, and where contradictions lie.
- **In OpenBrain the AI is primarily a READER.** Its job is to answer questions by pulling from structured data. When you or an agent ask something, it searches the carefully organised database, reads the relevant entries, and returns a precise, fresh synthesis based on all available data - analytical work on the fly, more detailed because all the detail is immediately available.

### Consequences

- **Writer (wiki):** you interact intensively when new information comes in. Adding one research paper can trigger updates across a dozen wiki pages - heavy on the front end - but afterwards answers are very cheap because the thinking is already captured.
- **Reader (OpenBrain):** adding information is lazy and cheap (write a row, tag it, done - "I'm a lazy person and I want my stuff auto-categorised as cheaply as possible"). The heavy operation is asking a question, because the AI reconstructs understanding from the data each time. Simple lookups are fast; complex ones take time as it interrogates the raw data. That cost recurs on similar questions - but you never lose detail when you need to get into the weeds.

---

## 8. Whose understanding matters? Provenance and trust

- **With a wiki**, when a colleague asks about a topic you are willing to check the wiki and trust what the AI says before answering. You are trusting that the AI's capture of your understanding is good enough to share as yours.
- **With OpenBrain**, provenance is clear: facts from identified sources with timestamps. You can trace any claim back to where it came from. You can speak with authority: "this is the raw material I got, these are the facts, this is a considered opinion from a query across all the data I have collected." That is a deeper, more consequential kind of trust.

**The highest-leverage document in the whole wiki system is the instruction file** that tells the AI how to organise and synthesise your wiki. If you are building a wiki, you are betting (in one Markdown file) that the AI will get organisation and accuracy right - or you invest time double-checking every ingest. Most people will under-invest, and the wiki will be worse than it should be. Not because it cannot be good - because we are lazy.

## 9. Where each approach wins

### Karpathy's wiki wins when...

- You are deep in research mode - reading ten papers on a topic over a couple of weeks (which sounds a lot like what Karpathy does; it is written for him).
- Each source builds on or contradicts the last. By paper five the wiki holds a synthesis of the first four, you have read the primaries yourself, and paper five integrates into the existing picture and evolves your thinking. Contradictions get flagged at ingest; cross-references build automatically.
- By paper ten you have a rich, navigable artifact representing the current state of your understanding of a difficult subject - "like NotebookLM on steroids", capturing not just current files but how your personal knowledge evolved over months (health, self-improvement, competitive analysis - any domain where value lies in the connections between sources rather than any single source).

### OpenBrain wins when...

- You need precise structured operations: "show me every meeting note from Q1 where pricing was discussed", "pull the three most recent competitor updates and compare them", "find all action items assigned to me in the last two weeks". These are database queries returning exact, filterable results. A folder of text files can only approximate this with keyword search - it misses things and breaks fast, especially when you combine filters, sort by date, or work across hundreds of entries.
- **Multi-agent access:** Claude Code, ChatGPT, Cursor, and a scheduled automation all reading/writing the same store at once. You need a database that handles simultaneous access, not a directory where two agents editing the same page create a mess.
- **Volume:** thousands of entries across dozens of categories with search, metadata, and relational queries. Karpathy acknowledges the wiki works best at roughly 100 to 10,000 high-signal documents - it is not corporate-level memory (do not use it as a company-wide context layer). At the top end (10,000 docs) you already need extra search tooling just to stay manageable. For thousands of contacts, transactions, events, tasks, and documents, structured storage is the only option that scales.

## 10. Where each system breaks

### The wiki breaks at scale (and with teams)

- Hit the wiki structure from multiple directions (person A vs person B, agent A vs agent B, all updating the same page) and you get conflicts, plus a weird merge that reflects nobody's deep personal understanding.
- The semantic understanding the wiki evolves is designed for a world like Karpathy's - a solo researcher thinking deeply, for himself. Solo practitioners are fine. Teams hit a serious problem.
- If knowledge changes daily (project status, competitive positioning, live deal flow), the cost of re-synthesising the wiki on every input becomes punishing - each change can ripple across multiple pages in uncontrollable ways. It should just be another row of data.
- Think of the wiki as optimised for papers-and-articles speed, not Slack-message-and-ticket-update speed. The biggest worry: people do not recognise that a knowledge system is designed to work at a particular speed of business, and may implement the wrong one.
- **Staleness differs.** A neglected database has gaps, but the old facts are still true - staleness looks like ignorance ("I forgot to put stuff in"). A neglected wiki drifts: old syntheses become increasingly wrong as new information is not integrated, yet still read with the confidence of well-written prose. Wiki staleness looks like active misinformation - you do not know you are wrong because the page reads like it knows what it is talking about, and you do not question the gap you cannot see.

### OpenBrain's scale breakpoints (and the fixes Nate is shipping)

- **Deep synthesis quality:** synthesising 15 facts at once, the AI can do it but somewhat unpredictably - it has no prior map of how it did this before, and searches the shelves from scratch each time. Usually good, but rarely as good as a pre-built synthesis with time to integrate everything deliberately. Being addressed.
- **Browsability:** OpenBrain is deliberately headless - no artifact to open and wander through - which gives flexibility to choose how you access it. It is easy to build the right "head" over the top; people have added Obsidian via a plugin. If you cannot browse the database, pick a plugin.
- **Contradictions:** in a wiki, contradictions surface at ingest (as long as your initial Markdown deliberately tells the AI to look for them). In a database, a contradiction can sit silently in adjacent rows unless you ask the right question. Nate is building a plugin to run audits that surface contradictions - using OpenBrain as a contradiction-surfacing tool so you can map the contradictions across a team or org from the raw material. Databases store facts and are not contradiction-aware by default, but in the age of AI it is relatively easy to extend one to be.

## 11. What both systems agree on (shared principles)

They disagree on implementation but share the underlying thesis:

- **You own the artifact, not the tool.** Karpathy's files are text in a folder you control; OpenBrain's data is in a database you own. Neither hands your knowledge to a platform that can re-price or lock you in. Karpathy calls it "file over app"; Nate calls it "building with no SaaS middlemen". Same conviction: in the age of AI, own your own context layer.
- **The human's job is curation and questioning.** You decide what sources go in and what questions to ask. There is no substitute for thinking carefully about how to organise your personal context layer.
- **Memory compounds through intentional structure, not random accumulation.** The only difference is where that structure lives (a wiki in Karpathy's case, a SQL database in OpenBrain's) and how it is positioned - to enable a certain kind of connection (between documents for the wiki; operational facts you can reason against and audit for OpenBrain).
- **The primary user of the knowledge base is an AI agent working on your behalf, not you reading in a browser.** Human readability is a bonus; agent accessibility is the requirement. Increasingly this will be the assumption of all memory systems.

## 12. The hybrid solution - a wiki graph over OpenBrain

The mature answer takes the strength of both. This is the next major OpenBrain extension:

- **Keep OpenBrain as your permanent store / durable memory layer.** Everything goes in - every meeting note, article clip, research finding, task, contact - tagged, searchable, queryable. It handles high volume, precise queries, recall across life domains. It is the source of truth.
- **Add a wiki layer as a compiled view on demand.** A new compilation-agent plugin runs on a schedule (daily, weekly, on demand). It reads OpenBrain's structured data, forms a graph of your knowledge base, synthesises across entries, and produces wiki pages and topic summaries. Think of it as a daily briefing a great chief of staff writes by reading everything and distilling it into something browsable.
- **Because it works from structured data, not raw files, it can do things Karpathy's ingest cannot:** filter entries by date or category before synthesising, weight by confidence, exclude outdated items. The synthesis is richer because the underlying data is more detailed. You can browse the resulting pages in Obsidian or any note app - a hot reference for when you are actively working a topic - all powered by a pre-built context graph living on your structured data.
- Unlike Karpathy's raw files, the underlying data is easily queryable, organised in SQL, and scales past the ~10,000-file ceiling.

### Why the hybrid avoids the error-compounding problem

- The database stays the single source of truth. New information always goes into the core SQL OpenBrain first. The wiki is never edited directly.
- Several commenters on Karpathy's post flagged error compounding: if the AI writes something slightly wrong into the wiki and it stays, the next answer builds on that wrong thing and drift accumulates.
- In the hybrid, the database is always authoritative and the wiki is generated from a graph built off it. If the wiki has an error, you fix the source data and regenerate. The wiki never drifts from reality because it is always rebuilt from ground truth in the database.
- In OpenBrain terms this is a "recipe": a composable workflow that queries relevant tables, synthesises pages through AI, builds a network of relationships, and writes output to a wiki directory. It can run on a schedule and gets better every cycle as the underlying data grows - a compounding loop, as long as you keep putting data in.

Net result: OpenBrain for structured storage and agent access, a Karpathy-style wiki over the top for compiled understanding and human browsability. The database feeds the wiki; the wiki never contradicts the database; you query whichever you need (precise fact vs synthesised narrative).

## 13. Blunt decision guide - which do you build?

**Use Karpathy's wiki (straight from his GitHub) if you are:**

- Going deep on a single research topic.
- A solo user.
- Not needing precise queries.
- Not needing multi-agent access.
- Wanting to think by reading and browsing.
- Wanting something running in 30 minutes with zero infrastructure (the AI builds the whole system for you; it is designed for exactly that solo use case).

**Build with OpenBrain if you:**

- Need multiple AI tools accessing the same memory.
- Assume a team works with the information.
- Capture high-volume information across many categories.
- Have data that is numbers-based rather than narrative-based.
- Need structured queries.
- Are building automated agent workflows.
- Are thinking about this as long-lived, scalable infrastructure, not a single project.

The wiki feels like a better, cooler NotebookLM - an amazing tool, but not one for an entire team.

**Nate's current recommendation: have it both ways.** Run OpenBrain, and if you want a browsable, pre-synthesised understanding layer, add the graph plugin over the top. Neither replaces the other, and you get the benefits of both.

## 14. Two ideas from Karpathy worth adopting immediately

Regardless of which system you pick:

1. **The idea file as a publishing format.** Karpathy did not ship a tool - he published a high-level description of an idea designed to be pasted into an AI agent that then builds the specifics with you. This is exactly what Nate means when he says "take my YouTube transcript and feed it to an AI". It is a genuinely new way to share technical knowledge: a great blueprint for an AI to execute, simpler than an exhaustive step-by-step a human must follow, and it respects the reader's agency - they add their own commentary and decide the details with the agent, starting from a proven pattern. (Yes - you can take this video's transcript and start your own memory project; just plug it into your agent and go.)

2. **Moving the AI from Oracle to maintainer.** Most people treat AI as something you ask questions of. Karpathy correctly treats it as something with an ongoing job - maintaining a knowledge artifact that gets better over time. AI is not for magical one-off answers from the clouds; it is for sustained work that compounds. The deeper insight: shift from an "answer engine" mindset to AI as a maintainer of thinking systems, so humans curate, think, select, and explore while the AI does the grunt work. That is the division of labour the AI dream promised.

## 15. Closing thought

The lesson from Karpathy's wiki is that you must become a thinker about how you want your memory and context layers to work in order to be an effective builder of agents and partner with AI through the second half of 2026 and into 2027. None of this excuses you from that thinking - it demands more of it. There is no substitute for making clear distinctions and clear decisions about how you want your knowledge structured, whether it is just you and a laptop, your team, or your org.

It is up to you to decide: structured data (because you need reliable queries above 10,000 artifacts), or the best of both (multi-agent structured results plus a graph that lets you think in connections between materials). If you are an engineer or product manager thinking about this in an org, you cannot substitute for that thoughtfulness. You have to do the thinking.
